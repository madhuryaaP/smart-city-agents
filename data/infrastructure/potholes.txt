POTHOLE REPORTING AND ROAD DAMAGE

A pothole is a depression or damaged section of a road surface that can affect road safety, vehicle movement, and the overall condition of the road.

Potholes usually develop when a section of pavement becomes weakened and breaks down. Traffic loading, rainfall, water entering cracks, poor drainage, ageing pavement, construction activity, and inadequate maintenance can contribute to pothole formation.

Potholes can vary in size, depth, shape, and severity. Some may be small surface defects, while larger potholes can affect a significant portion of the road surface.

CAUSES OF POTHOLES

Repeated traffic is one of the main factors that can contribute to the development and growth of potholes. Vehicles repeatedly apply loads to the pavement, and weakened sections can deteriorate over time.

Heavy vehicles can place greater loads on pavement. Roads that experience frequent heavy traffic require suitable pavement strength and regular maintenance.

Water is another important factor in pothole formation. Rainwater can enter cracks or damaged portions of pavement. When water remains in or around the pavement, it can contribute to weakening of the affected area.

Poor drainage can increase the likelihood of recurring road damage. If rainwater cannot drain away effectively, water may accumulate near the road and contribute to pavement deterioration.

Ageing pavement can also become more vulnerable to cracking and surface deterioration. If defects are not addressed in time, they can develop into larger damaged areas.

Construction and utility work can also affect road surfaces. Excavation for water pipelines, drainage, electrical cables, telecommunications, or other utilities can disturb pavement. If the road is not properly restored, the affected section may deteriorate and develop defects.

HOW POTHOLES DEVELOP

Pothole formation can begin with small cracks or weaknesses in the road surface.

Water can enter these damaged areas and weaken the pavement. Continued traffic over the weakened section can cause the damaged area to break down further.

As deterioration progresses, a localized depression can form and gradually become a pothole.

The rate at which a pothole develops depends on factors such as traffic volume, vehicle loading, rainfall, drainage conditions, pavement condition, construction quality, and the severity of the original defect.

WHY DO POTHOLES GET BIGGER?

Potholes can become larger when vehicles repeatedly pass over the damaged section.

Rainfall and water accumulation can further affect the weakened pavement. If the surrounding pavement is also deteriorating, the damaged area may continue to expand.

A pothole that repeatedly appears in the same location may indicate an underlying problem such as poor drainage, pavement deterioration, or an issue associated with previous road or utility work.

EFFECTS OF POTHOLES

Potholes can affect the smooth movement of vehicles and may require drivers to slow down or change their path.

Large or deep potholes may create greater safety concerns, particularly on roads with heavy traffic or at locations where visibility is limited.

Potholes can also affect two-wheelers, cyclists, and other road users.

Multiple potholes along a road can reduce the usable quality of the road and may contribute to slower traffic movement.

POTHOLE MAINTENANCE

Pothole repair is part of routine road maintenance. The appropriate repair method depends on the size, depth, severity, surrounding pavement condition, and underlying cause of the pothole.

Repairing the visible pothole may not permanently solve the problem if the underlying cause is poor drainage, structural pavement deterioration, or another infrastructure issue.

Regular inspection and timely maintenance can help identify potholes before they become more extensive.

If potholes repeatedly occur at the same location, the responsible authority may need to investigate the underlying cause in addition to repairing the surface.

POTHOLE REPORTING

When reporting a pothole, useful information includes:

- Exact or approximate location
- Nearby landmark
- Description of the road damage
- Photograph of the pothole when possible
- Whether the pothole is affecting traffic movement
- Approximate size or severity when it can be safely observed
- Whether water is accumulating around the pothole, if applicable

Clear location information helps the responsible authority identify the affected road section.

A photograph can help the responsible authority understand the condition and approximate severity of the pothole.

Reports should clearly mention whether the pothole is affecting traffic movement or is located in an area with frequent pedestrian or vehicle movement.

Photographs should only be taken when it is safe to do so and should not require a person to stand in moving traffic.

LARGE OR SEVERE POTHOLES

Large or deep potholes may create greater safety concerns and should be reported promptly.

A pothole that occupies a significant part of a traffic lane, occurs at an intersection, affects a heavily used road, or is difficult for road users to avoid may require greater attention from the responsible authority.

Water-filled potholes may also be difficult to assess visually because the actual depth and condition of the damaged area may not be immediately visible.

GENERAL POTHOLE KNOWLEDGE

Potholes are one of several forms of road deterioration. Other forms include cracks, rutting, uneven surfaces, damaged road edges, and broken pavement.

Potholes can be caused by a combination of factors rather than a single factor.

Rainfall does not necessarily create a pothole by itself. Water can contribute to deterioration when pavement is already weakened or when drainage is inadequate.

Road condition can change over time because of traffic, rainfall, construction activity, and continued deterioration.

The exact cause of a pothole cannot always be determined from its appearance alone. The responsible authority may need to inspect the road and surrounding infrastructure before selecting the appropriate repair method.
