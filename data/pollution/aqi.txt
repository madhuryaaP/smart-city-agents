AIR QUALITY INDEX (AQI)

The Air Quality Index, commonly called AQI, is used to communicate air pollution conditions in a way that is easier for the public to understand.

AQI converts air-quality measurements into an index or category that helps communicate the general condition of the air.

AQI information can be used to communicate whether air quality is generally good, moderate, poor, very poor, or severe according to the applicable AQI system.

AQI CATEGORIES

AQI categories provide a simple way to describe the level of air pollution.

Depending on the AQI standard being used, categories may range from relatively good air quality to increasingly polluted or severe conditions.

The meaning and numerical range of each category can differ between AQI standards and countries.

AQI values should therefore always be interpreted according to the specific AQI standard and data source being used by the application.

AQI AND POLLUTANTS

AQI is based on measurements of one or more air pollutants.

Common pollutants considered in air-quality assessment include:

- PM2.5
- PM10
- Nitrogen dioxide
- Sulfur dioxide
- Carbon monoxide
- Ozone

The pollutant contributing most significantly to the reported AQI can depend on the location, time, measurement conditions, and AQI calculation method.

A high AQI does not necessarily mean that every individual pollutant is at the same level.

AQI VALUES

AQI values provide a simplified representation of air-quality conditions.

A lower AQI generally represents better air-quality conditions within a particular AQI system, while a higher AQI generally represents greater pollution.

The numerical meaning of an AQI value depends on the standard used to calculate it.

For this reason, an AQI value should not be interpreted without knowing the applicable AQI scale or data source.

AQI AND AIR QUALITY

AQI provides a convenient way to communicate air-quality conditions, but it is different from directly reporting the concentration of an individual pollutant.

For example, PM2.5 concentration and AQI are not the same measurement.

Pollutant concentration represents the measured amount of a particular pollutant, while AQI communicates air-quality conditions using an index.

AQI can therefore make technical air-quality information easier for general users to understand.

AQI CHANGES OVER TIME

AQI can change throughout the day as pollutant concentrations change.

Traffic activity, industrial activity, construction, weather conditions, wind, rainfall, sunlight, and other factors can influence air-quality measurements.

AQI can therefore be different at different times even within the same location.

AQI can also vary between different locations in the same city.

AQI AND LOCATION

AQI measurements represent air-quality conditions associated with a particular monitoring location and time.

An AQI value from one monitoring station may not accurately represent conditions throughout an entire city.

Different areas can have different AQI values because of differences in traffic, local pollution sources, weather, land use, and other environmental conditions.

AQI DATA SOURCES

AQI information can come from air-quality monitoring stations, sensors, environmental monitoring systems, or other data sources.

The reliability and interpretation of AQI information depend on the quality, location, time, and source of the underlying measurements.

The application should identify or maintain information about the source of AQI data when presenting air-quality information to users.

AQI IN SMART CITY APPLICATIONS

The Smart City application can use air-quality measurements and AQI information to provide understandable pollution-related information.

AQI can help users quickly understand the general air-quality condition of a location without requiring them to interpret several individual pollutant measurements.

The application can combine AQI information with pollutant measurements, location, time, and general environmental information to provide clearer context.

AQI information can also support urban monitoring by helping identify areas or periods with changing air-quality conditions.

INTERPRETING AQI

AQI should be interpreted according to the specific AQI standard being used.

Different countries and organizations may use different AQI calculation methods, pollutant breakpoints, category names, and numerical ranges.

Therefore, the same numerical AQI value should not automatically be assumed to have exactly the same meaning under every AQI system.

The application should use the appropriate interpretation for the AQI standard and data source associated with its measurements.

GENERAL AQI KNOWLEDGE

AQI is a communication tool for describing air-quality conditions.

It does not represent only one pollutant unless the particular AQI calculation identifies that pollutant as the dominant or relevant contributor.

AQI can change because pollutant concentrations and environmental conditions change.

A single AQI value represents conditions for a particular location and measurement period and should not automatically be treated as the air quality of an entire city.

AQI information is most useful when considered together with the measurement location, measurement time, applicable AQI standard, and underlying pollutant data.
